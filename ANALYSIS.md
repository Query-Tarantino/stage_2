# Stage 1 benchmark analysis

This document interprets the full benchmark run of October 2, 2026 (`benchmarks/report/comparison.md`, generated by
`scripts/compare_results.py` from `benchmarks/results/java-*.csv`). It answers four questions:

- why each structure gets its numbers, given the complexity of its operations;
- whether the benchmark is representative;
- what would happen with tens of thousands, hundreds of thousands and a million books;
- which structure to choose for each component.

The rule for choosing is: **the structure with the best complexity in the operations that grow with the number of books;
on a tie, the one with the best measured constants.**

`benchmarks/` is not in git, so this document keeps the numbers it relies on.

## 1. Summary

| Component | Choice | By complexity | What the measurements say |
|---|---|---|---|
| Datalake | `batch` | Stores and looks up in O(1), with O(N/1 000) directories | Ties with `book` (555 against 526 books/s). `time` looks up in O(N): 2.1 ms per book with 1 000 books |
| Inverted index | `mongo` | Never worse than the other two; memory, opening and freshness in O(1) | `json` wins 14 of the 18 metrics with 1 000 books, but its memory, opening and reloading are O(P): ~40 GB of memory with 100 000 books |
| Metadata | `sqlite` | Same as `mongo` in every operation | Reads 10 times faster (4.6 against 48 µs by id) |

Three findings that `comparison.md` does not show:

1. **All three index structures rewrite almost the whole index on every batch.** A batch of 100 books touches only 14% of
   the terms, but those terms (among them every frequent one) hold 86% of the postings. Updating costs O(N) per batch in
   all three, and reaching N books costs O(N²).
2. **Queries have the same complexity in all three.** Copying each list into a `TreeSet` in `SearchCommand` dominates; it
   costs O(df·log df) and is shared by all three. With 100 000 books, a frequent query would take 6.5 ms (json), 8.4 ms
   (folders) and 9.6 ms (mongo).
3. **In a real search, metadata dominates,** because it is fetched one result at a time. With 100 000 books, a frequent
   query would spend 0.42 s (sqlite) or 4.4 s (mongo) reading metadata, against ~10 ms in the index.

## 2. Data, method and notation

### 2.1 The run

- **Machine:** fanless MacBook Air M4, 24 GB of RAM, SSD, macOS (APFS).
- **Software:** Java 25, with 4 GB of memory per benchmark process (`-Xms4g -Xmx4g`). MongoDB 7.0 running natively on the
  same machine, with a 1 GB cache.
- **Sizes:** 100, 300 and 1 000 books from `benchmarks/cache/`, in the order of `workload/book_ids.txt`. Updates add the next
  100 books (positions 1 001–1 100).
- **JMH:** two passes of one process each; the second runs the parameters in reverse order. Each value is the mean ± the
  half-width of its 95% confidence interval. Two structures tie when their intervals overlap.
- **Duration:** about 2 h 10 min (crawler 7 min, indexer 1 h 52 min, query 10 min).
- **Correctness:** the benchmark checks that the three structures hold the same vocabulary and that every query returns the
  expected books before measuring it.

### 2.2 Notation

| Symbol | Meaning | With 1 000 books |
|---|---|---:|
| N | books indexed | 1 000 |
| V(N) | distinct terms (vocabulary) | 434 996 |
| P(N) | postings: (term, book) pairs | 6 425 580 |
| df(t) | books that contain term t | ≤ N; ~924 for frequent terms |
| q | terms in a query | 1–4 |

### 2.3 How V and P grow

The vocabulary of the 2 800 cached books was recomputed with the same rules as `Tokenizer`:

- body between the Gutenberg markers;
- lowercase text;
- runs of letters of at least 2 characters;
- stopwords removed.

The recount reproduces the benchmark's `term_count` exactly: 106 410, 224 350 and 434 996 terms.

| N | 100 | 300 | 1 000 | 2 000 | 2 800 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| V(N) | 106 410 | 224 350 | 434 996 | 587 545 | 755 458 |
| P(N) | 625 651 | 1 882 575 | 6 425 580 | 12 241 147 | 17 024 158 |
| P(N) / N | 6 257 | 6 275 | 6 426 | 6 121 | 6 080 |

- **Heaps' law:** V(N) ≈ 7 878·N^0.575, fitted on 10 sizes between 100 and 2 800 books. With only N ≥ 1 000 the exponent is
  0.543. The vocabulary grows more slowly than N: each new book adds fewer and fewer new terms.
- **Postings:** P(N) ≈ 6 200·N (fitted exponent 0.987). Each book adds about 6 200 postings, one per distinct term it
  contains.
- **Zipf's law:** with 1 000 books, 54% of the terms appear in a single book, and a few terms hold almost all the postings.
  No term appears in every book; the most common one is in 961 of 1 000.

| Terms that appear in… | How many | % of the vocabulary | % of the postings |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| ≥ 50% of the books | 2 396 | 0.6% | 25% |
| ≥ 10% of the books | 14 865 | 3.4% | 68% |
| ≥ 1% of the books | 54 919 | 12.6% | 88% |

- **A batch of 100 new books** touches 78 643 terms. Of these, 16 855 are new and 61 788 were already in the index: 14% of
  the vocabulary. Those 61 788 terms hold **86% of the postings**, because every frequent term is among them. This decides
  the complexity of updates (section 5).

### 2.4 How the estimates are made

Each estimate is the complexity of the operation with its measured constants. For example, building the index costs
c_V·V(N) + c_P·P(N): a cost per term plus a cost per posting.

- The constants are fitted with 100 and 1 000 books.
- Each model is validated by predicting 300 books, a size not used to fit it. Errors range from 1% to 9%.
- Larger sizes use the V(N) and P(N) of section 2.3.

Curves are not fitted blindly. With 1 000 books fixed costs weigh a lot, and a curve y = a·N^b fitted to the three points
is misleading. For example, fitted to the query time, it predicts that with a million books mongo would answer more than
200 times faster than json. With the complexity model, a frequent query takes ~80 ms in json and ~110 ms in mongo.

The figures for 100 000 and 1 000 000 books are orders of magnitude, not exact predictions. Project Gutenberg has about
75 000 books: the 100 000 column is all of Gutenberg, and the million column assumes other sources.

## 3. Is the benchmark representative?

**Yes, for comparing these three implementations up to 1 000 books:**

- all of them process the same books with the same vocabulary, and this is checked;
- JMH measures with warm-up, two processes, confidence intervals and reverse order in the second pass;
- the constants it measures (cost per term, per posting and per file) are what make extrapolation possible.

**No, for a product with tens of thousands of books or more:**

1. **Everything fits in memory.** With 1 000 books, json takes 445 MB of Java memory and mongo 58 MB inside its 1 GB cache.
   With 100 000 books none of that fits, and disk reads and memory exhaustion appear, which the benchmark never sees.
2. **Queries always touch the same 30 terms.** The 30 queries in `workload/queries.txt` use 30 distinct terms, so their
   lists (or their 30 files, in folders) are always cached. A real search engine receives a wide variety of terms.
3. **Fixed costs weigh too much.** mongo's 47 µs per term dominate with 1 000 books and are negligible with 100 000
   (section 5.5).
4. **MongoDB runs on the same machine.** In production it usually runs on another one, and each round trip would go from
   ~0.05 ms to a few tenths of a millisecond.
5. **One query is measured at a time, without metadata or HTTP.** There are no concurrent queries and no queries while
   indexing, and the complete search is not measured: `query_time` uses constant metadata on purpose.
6. **The full build keeps the whole index in memory before writing it,** in all three structures (`build_memory`:
   415–418 MB with 1 000 books). At about 10 000 books it would no longer fit in the 4 GB of the benchmark processes, so the
   benchmark could not run at that size without changes. The real pipeline writes every 100 books, so that memory only
   grows without bound with json.
7. **It compares implementations, not technologies.** Here "json" means "the whole index in memory, loaded at once and
   rewritten whole", and "mongo" means "one document per term that grows with every book". Another design with the same
   technology would give other numbers.
8. **It was measured on a fanless laptop.** The indexer's second pass took twice as long as the first (section 8).

For Stage 1 this is enough, as long as the report states these limits and accompanies the results with this scale
analysis.

## 4. Datalake

### 4.1 How each structure stores the books

- `time`: `YYYYMMDD/HH/<id>.header.txt` and `<id>.body.txt`, by download hour (the layout of the assignment, PDF §3).
- `book`: one directory per book, `<id>/header.txt` and `body.txt`.
- `batch`: one directory per 1 000 ids, `<id div 1000>/<id>.header.txt` and `<id>.body.txt`.

Before storing a book, the crawler checks whether it is already stored (`IngestBookCommand.stored`), so storing includes a
lookup.

### 4.2 Complexity

H is the number of hours with downloads: it grows with how long the crawl runs, not with N.

| Operation | time | book | batch | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Look up a book | O(N) | O(1) | O(1) | `time` does not know in which hour the book was stored and walks the whole tree (`Files.find`). `book` and `batch` compute the path from the id |
| Store a book | O(N) | O(1) | O(1) | Includes the lookup above |
| Detect new books | O(H + new) | O(N) | O(N) | `time` lists only the hour directories and reads those after the last check. `book` and `batch` check the date of every book |
| Recover from a crash | O(N) | O(N) | O(N) | All three walk the datalake (`Files.walk`) looking for half-written files |
| Directories | O(H) | O(N) | O(N/1 000) | |
| Files and disk | O(N) | O(N) | O(N) | Two files per book with the same content |

### 4.3 The measurements confirm the complexity

| | N = 100 | N = 300 | N = 1 000 | With 10 times more books |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Look up, `time` | 210 µs | 601 µs | 2 103 µs | ×10.0: O(N) |
| Look up, `batch` | 0.48 µs | 0.52 µs | 0.59 µs | ×1.2: O(1) |
| Look up, `book` | 0.53 µs | 0.54 µs | 0.83 µs | ×1.6: O(1) |
| Store, `time` | 347 books/s | 389 books/s | 280 books/s | Drops once the lookup starts to weigh |
| Store, `batch` | 369 books/s | 498 books/s | 555 books/s | Does not drop |
| Detect new, `time` | 0.15 ms | 0.14 ms | 0.17 ms | Constant |
| Detect new, `batch` | 0.51 ms | 0.98 ms | 2.76 ms | Grows with N |

- **Recovery:** all three structures recover correctly from a crash (`recovery_ok` = 1 and no leftover files, with 100
  books).
- **Disk:** they take the same space, 454 MB with 1 000 books.

### 4.5 Choice: `batch`

- **Complexity:** stores and looks up in O(1), like `book`, with far fewer directories: O(N/1 000) against O(N). For all of
  Gutenberg that would be about 75 directories instead of 75 000.
- **Why not `time`:** its lookup is O(N). To store 100 000 books it would spend ~3 hours just looking up, and its write rate
  would fall below 5 books/s.
- **`time`'s only advantage is not needed:** detecting new books in O(H + new). The control service already queues each book
  for indexing as soon as it finishes downloading. To detect new books without the control layer, a manifest (a file to
  which each stored id is appended) would be enough, and detection would become O(new) with any structure.

## 5. Inverted index

### 5.1 How each structure stores the index

- **json** (`MonolithicJsonIndexAdapter`): the whole index in memory, as a `TreeMap<String, TreeSet<Integer>>`. Every write
  (`flush`) serializes the whole index to a single JSON file with Jackson. The reader (`MonolithicJsonIndexReader`) loads the
  whole file the first time and never reads it again.
- **folders** (`FolderPerTermIndexAdapter`): one file per term, one id per line, inside one directory per first letter. For
  every term touched, each write reads its whole file, adds the new ids, writes it whole to a temporary file and renames it.
- **mongo** (`MongodbIndexAdapter`): one `{term, postings[]}` document per term, with a unique index on `term`. Each write
  sends a `bulkWrite` with one `$addToSet` per term touched, and MongoDB compares the new ids with those already in the
  array.
- **Query** (`SearchCommand`, shared by all three): for each term it fetches its list, copies it into a `TreeSet` and
  intersects with `retainAll`. Then it fetches the metadata of every result, one by one.

### 5.2 Complexity

Σ_touched df is the number of postings of the terms a batch touches: ≈ 0.86·P (section 2.3).

| Operation | json | folders | mongo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build with N books | O(P·log N) | O(P·log N + V·c_fs) | O(P·log N + V·log V) |
| Update a batch of K books | O(P + V) | O(T_batch·c_fs + Σ_touched df) | at least O(T_batch·log V + Σ_touched df) |
| Index N books in batches | O(N²/K) | O(N²/K) | at least O(N²/K) |
| Open or reload the index | O(P + V) | O(1) | O(1) |
| Memory in the JVM | O(P + V) | O(1) | O(1) |
| Disk | O(P + V) | O(P + V·4 KB) | O(P + V) |
| Number of files | 1 | V | MongoDB's own, independent of V |
| Query with q terms (current algorithm) | O(Σ df·log df) | O(Σ (c_fs + df·log df)) | O(Σ (log V + df·log df)) |
| See new books | Reload: O(P + V) | O(1) | O(1) |

mongo's bound is "at least" because how MongoDB implements `$addToSet` internally has not been checked. At minimum it has
to scan the array.

What matters in the table:

- **Updating is O(N) per batch in all three.**
  - json rewrites everything (P + V).
  - folders and mongo only touch the batch's terms, but those terms hold 86% of the postings, so Σ_touched df ≈ 0.86·P.
  - The advantage the assignment attributes to folders ("only the file of the affected term is modified") barely helps: a
    batch always touches the frequent terms, and their lists grow with N.
  - Indexing N books in batches costs O(N²/K) in all three; only the constants differ.
- **Memory, opening and freshness separate json from the other two:** O(P + V) against O(1).
- **Queries have the same complexity in all three.** `SearchCommand` copies each whole list into a `TreeSet`, which costs
  O(df·log df) per term: much more than reaching the term (O(1) in json, O(log V) in mongo).

### 5.3 Measured constants

| Constant | json | folders | mongo | Validation with N = 300 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Build: per term | 4.3 µs | 147 µs | 14.6 µs | +2% to +3% |
| Build: per posting | 2.1 µs | 3.6 µs | 2.5 µs | |
| Query: fixed cost per term | 0.37 µs | 11.8 µs | 47.3 µs | −7% to +1% |
| Query: reading each id of the list | — | 20.9 ns | 32.7 ns | |
| Query: copying the list into a `TreeSet` (shared) | 4.25 ns × df·log₂ df | same | same | |
| Memory in the JVM | 91 B per term + 63 B per posting | 0 | 0 | −4% |
| Disk | 3.9 B per term + 4.6 B per posting | 4 KB per term + data | 21 B per term + 7.6 B per posting | −2% to −4% |
| Update a batch: fixed part | 14.4 ms per book | 33.8 s per batch | 45 ms per book | |
| Update a batch: per posting rewritten | 19.3 ns (whole index) | 1.39 µs (the 86% touched) | Not measurable | folders: +6% |

What the constants say:

- **folders pays ~147 µs per term file** (creating, writing and renaming it). With 434 996 terms that is 64 of the 87 s it
  takes to build the index.
- **mongo pays ~47 µs per term on every query.** That is the round trip to MongoDB: the same cost as looking up a book's
  metadata by id in mongo (48 µs).
- **json takes 63 B of memory per posting**, because each one is an `Integer` inside a `TreeSet` or `HashSet`. In the file it
  takes 4.6 B: 14 times less.
- **json rewrites at ~250 MB/s** (19.3 ns per posting). That is fast, but it rewrites the whole index on every batch.
- **The per-posting cost of mongo's updates cannot be measured:** with ≤ 1 000 books it stays within the noise (32–45 ms per
  book, with no trend). The estimates use json's as an upper bound.

| | json | folders | mongo |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Full build | 15.6 ± 3.6 s | 86.8 ± 26.1 s | 22.5 ± 0.9 s |
| Update book by book | 133 ms/book | 3 607 ms/book | 379 ms/book |
| Update in batches of 100 | 15.6 ms/book | 415 ms/book | 44.9 ms/book |
| Open the index | 507 ms | 0.69 ms | 1.38 ms |

- folders allocates exactly 434 996 × 4 KB = 1 781.7 MB: one block per term file, 70 times the size of its data.
- `build_memory` (415–418 MB) is the same in all three because it measures the same in-memory structure before writing. json
  "winning" by 0.7% has no practical meaning.
- Indexing in batches of 100 makes each book about 8.5 times cheaper in all three.
- The "0" memory of folders and mongo means the memory is outside the JVM: in the file system cache and in MongoDB's cache
  (up to 1 GB).

The query categories show that the cost adds up term by term:

| Query (µs) | df of its terms | json | folders | mongo |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `rare` | 1–3 | 0.37 | 11.8 | 47.3 |
| `empty` (2 rare) | 1–3 each | 0.53 | 23.0 | 93.9 |
| `frequent` | ~924 | 39.0 | 69.8 | 116 |
| `mixed` (frequent + rare) | ~924 and 1–3 | 39.0 | 82.4 | 165 |
| `long` (4 common) | 414–957 | 170 | 294 | 469 |

`empty` ≈ 2 × `rare`, and `mixed` ≈ `frequent` + `rare`, even though `mixed` returns a few books and `frequent` about 924.
This is what O(Σ df·log df) predicts: the cost does not depend on the size of the result, and `SearchCommand` does not take
advantage of the rare term.

### 5.6 What the estimates show

1. **Memory is json's limit.** The services set no maximum memory, so Java uses a quarter of the RAM: 6 GB on a 24 GB
   machine.
   - The query service with json would run out of memory at around 13 000–15 000 books, and the indexer keeps another full
     copy of the index.
   - With 100 000 books, each copy would need ~40 GB.
2. **Indexing in batches costs O(N²) in all three, but with very different constants.** To reach 100 000 books:
   - json would take ~2 h, if it had 40 GB of memory;
   - mongo, between 1.2 and 2.7 h;
   - folders, ~5 days: it pays ~0.43 ms per file touched and 1.4 µs per posting rewritten, about 70 times more than json.
3. **Queries end up even.** With 100 000 books, a frequent query takes 6.5 ms (json), 8.4 ms (folders) and 9.6 ms (mongo):
   the copy into a `TreeSet`, shared by all three, dominates. json's advantage (0.37 µs per term against 47 µs) disappears
   when the lists are long.
4. **folders does not scale in number of files.** With 100 000 books there would be 5–6 million files, with hundreds of
   thousands in the directory of each common letter.
5. **mongo holds up to ~1.4 million books.** Beyond that, a term present in almost every book would exceed the 16 MB a
   MongoDB document allows. Until then, its JVM memory, opening and freshness do not depend on N.

### 5.8 Choice: `mongo`

Complexity per operation (= is the same as the best of the three):

| Operation | json | folders | mongo |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Build | = | = | = |
| Update in batches | = | = | = |
| Query (current algorithm) | = | = | = |
| Open or reload | worse, O(P + V) | = | = |
| Memory in the JVM | worse, O(P + V) | = | = |
| See new books | worse, O(P + V) | = | = |
| Number of files | = | worse, O(V) | = |

- **mongo never has worse complexity than the other two.**
- **json is worse in three operations,** the ones that prevent using it at scale: memory, opening and freshness.
- **folders is worse in number of files,** and also has the worst write constants (up to 27 times slower than json).

Between json and mongo, json has better constants, but the rule puts complexity first. Besides, json cannot reach the sizes
where those constants would matter.

Only one operation would favor json with a better algorithm: intersecting starting from the shortest list and checking its
ids in the others, which costs O(q·df_min) if the lists are in memory. folders and mongo, as they are, have to read each
whole list: O(Σ df). That is why the target design combines both (section 9).

## 6. Metadata

### 6.1 How each one stores the metadata

- **sqlite:** table `books` with `book_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY` (a B-tree). Each book is stored with an `INSERT OR REPLACE` in
  autocommit mode, that is, one transaction, with its write synced to disk, per book.
- **mongo:** collection `books` with a unique index on `book_id`. Each book is stored with a `replaceOne` with upsert.
- **Books by an author:** sqlite uses `WHERE author LIKE '%author%'`, and mongo an unanchored, case-insensitive regular
  expression. Neither search can use an index, and there is no index on `author` either.

### 6.2 Complexity

r is the number of books returned.

| Operation | sqlite | mongo |
|---|---|---|
| Book by id | O(log N) | O(log N) |
| Books by an author ("contains") | O(N) | O(N) |
| Books by an author with an index (exact or prefix) | O(log N + r) | O(log N + r) |
| Store a book | O(log N) + one transaction with a synced write | O(log N) + one round trip |

They have the same complexity in every operation, so the constants decide.

### 6.3 Measured constants

| | sqlite | mongo |
|---|---:|---:|
| Book by id (does not change with N) | 4.6 µs | 48 µs |
| Books by an author: fixed part + each book scanned | 7.4 µs + 52 ns | 34 µs + 590 ns |
| Store 1 000 books | 461 ± 321 ms | 105 ± 14 ms |

- **By id:** sqlite runs in the same process; mongo pays the round trip (~45 µs).
- **By author:** sqlite scans the table 11 times faster.
- **Storing is not a fair comparison:**
  - sqlite confirms each book with a write synced to disk;
  - MongoDB, with its default configuration, acknowledges before writing its journal;
  - with one transaction per batch, sqlite would take a few milliseconds;
  - besides, 0.1–0.5 s for 1 000 books is negligible next to the 15–87 s it takes to index them.

### 6.4 Estimates

| | 1 000 (measured) | 10 000 | 20 000 | 100 000 | 1 000 000 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Book by id, sqlite | 4.6 µs | ~4.6 µs | ~4.6 µs | ~4.6 µs | ~4.6 µs |
| Book by id, mongo | 48 µs | ~48 µs | ~48 µs | ~48 µs | ~48 µs |
| Books by an author, sqlite | 60 µs | 0.53 ms | 1.1 ms | 5.3 ms | 53 ms |
| Books by an author, mongo | 624 µs | 5.9 ms | 12 ms | 59 ms | 590 ms |
| Store N books, sqlite (autocommit) | 0.46 s | 4.6 s | 9.2 s | 46 s | 7.7 min |
| Store N books, mongo | 0.1 s | 1.1 s | 2.1 s | 11 s | 1.8 min |

### 6.5 Choice: `sqlite`

sqlite has the same complexity as mongo in every operation, reads 10 times faster and needs no server.

Recommended changes:

- One transaction per batch when storing.
- A column with the normalized author and an index on it, or FTS5 to search words of the author or title. Searching by
  author then goes from O(N) to O(log N + r).

**Limit: one writer at a time.** SQLite locks the whole database file to write, so only one process writes at a time.
That is enough today, because the control layer is the only one that writes metadata (SPEC §9). With several indexers
writing at once, as in Stage 2 or with several containers:

- **On the same machine**, their writes queue up: each one waits for the lock, or fails with `SQLITE_BUSY` if no
  `busy_timeout` is set. WAL mode lets reads go on during a write, but there is still only one writer.
- **On several machines**, they cannot share the file: SQLite's locks are not reliable over a network file system, and
  the database can be corrupted.

For a distributed architecture, the logical long-term step is to move the metadata to **PostgreSQL**. Like MongoDB it
is a server, but relational, and with row locks and MVCC many processes write and read at the same time. It keeps the
same complexity: a B-tree on `book_id`, and `INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE` for the upsert. A trigram index (`pg_trgm`)
would also let the "contains" search by author use an index instead of scanning the table. The price is the one mongo
pays here: one network round trip per query, so a book by id would cost about mongo's 48 µs rather than sqlite's 4.6 µs.
Fetching each page's metadata in a single query (section 7) makes that negligible. PostgreSQL has not been measured
here: it should be benchmarked as a third metadata backend before switching.

## 7. The complete search

With the current design, a query costs:

T(query) ≈ Σ per term (fixed cost + reading per id × df + 4.25 ns × df·log₂ df) + R × (metadata of one book by id)

With 100 000 books, a query for one frequent term (df ≈ R ≈ 92 400) splits like this:

| Part | json + sqlite | mongo + sqlite | mongo + mongo |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Index | 6.5 ms | 9.6 ms | 9.6 ms |
| Metadata of every result | 0.42 s | 0.42 s | 4.4 s |
| Total | ~0.43 s | ~0.43 s | ~4.4 s |

If results are paginated (for example, 20 per page) and the page's metadata is fetched in a single query, metadata costs one
query (~0.1 ms) and the index dominates again. That is why pagination is the improvement with the most impact for Stage 2.

## 8. Reliability of the measurements

- **Queries:** the most stable measurements (±1–3%). They ran separately, in 10 minutes.
- **Indexer:**
  - The second pass took twice as long as the first (75 against 37 min). The likely causes are heat on a fanless laptop or
    the state of the disk after creating and deleting millions of small files; this has not been checked.
  - The second pass runs in reverse order to cancel that drift in the mean, but the intervals of json and folders in build
    and update end up between ±20% and ±60%.
  - The winners do not change: with 1 000 books the build intervals do not overlap (json 12.0–19.2 s, mongo 21.6–23.4 s,
    folders 60.7–112.9 s).
- **Not checked:**
  - whether mongo gets slower between iterations of the same trial (the CSV files only keep the mean of each pass);
  - the per-posting cost of mongo's updates, which stays within the noise with ≤ 1 000 books.
- **Estimates:** those for 100 000 and 1 000 000 books extrapolate one or two orders of magnitude. They are useful to compare
  structures and find limits, not as exact predictions.

## 9. Conclusions

Rule: best complexity in the operations that grow with N; on a tie, best constants.

| Component | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Datalake | `batch` | Stores and looks up in O(1), with O(N/1 000) directories. New books are detected with a manifest in O(new) |
| Inverted index | `mongo` | Never worse in complexity than the other two; memory, opening and freshness in O(1). Holds up to ~1.4 million books |
| Metadata | `sqlite` | Same complexity as mongo, 10 times faster and without a server |

Improvements for Stage 2, ordered by impact:

| Improvement | Operation | Now | After |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paginate and fetch the page's metadata in one query (`IN`, `$in`) | Metadata of a search | O(R) queries | 1 query for the k books of the page |
| Intersect starting from the shortest list | Query | O(Σ df·log df) | O(q·df_min) with the lists in memory |
| Store mongo's postings in buckets (`{term, bucket, ids}`) and append with `$push` to the last one | Update a batch | O(T_batch·log V + Σ_touched df) | O(T_batch·log V), with no 16 MB limit |
| sqlite: one transaction per batch, and an index on the normalized author or FTS5 | Store and search by author | One transaction per book; O(N) | One transaction per batch; O(log N + r) |
| `batch` datalake with a manifest | Detect new books | O(N) | O(new) |
| When several processes or machines index at once, PostgreSQL instead of sqlite for the metadata (section 6.5) | Write metadata concurrently | One writer at a time, on one machine | Many writers on any machine, at one network round trip per query |
| In the longer term, a segment-based index like Lucene's: one immutable segment per batch, background merges and memory-mapped files | Update and query | O(N) per batch | Proportional to the batch's postings, plus O(P·log N) of merges in total, with bounded memory |
